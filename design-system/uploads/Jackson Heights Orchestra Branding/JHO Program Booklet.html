Our Mission

The Jackson Heights Orchestra is dedicated to enriching and elevating the dynamic cultural landscape of the Jackson Heights community through live performance. Our ensemble is composed of musicians from diverse backgrounds, united by a shared passion for music-making.

About the JHO

Established in 2012 by founding Music Director Patricia Glunt in collaboration with the Jackson Heights Beautification Group. During her tenure from 2012 to 2023, Glunt grew the ensemble into one of the premiere orchestras of Queens.

Notes on the Program

Overture in C Major (1832)

Fanny Mendelssohn-Hensel

Fanny Mendelssohn-Hensel's Overture in C Major is one of those pieces that makes you wonder what we lost because the 19th century was so wrong about women.

Born into the brilliant Mendelssohn family, Fanny was every bit as musically gifted as her younger brother Felix. A formidable pianist, composer, conductor, and musical organizer, she was also the gravitational center of an extraordinary artistic circle in Berlin. Unlike Felix, she was not encouraged toward a public career. She composed over 300 lieder and piano works, performed largely in private salons.

The Overture is her only surviving orchestral work. Probably written between 1830 and 1832, it was discovered over 100 years after her death. Opening with poise and expectancy, the music quickly reveals her grasp of orchestral color, structure, and drama. There is brightness, but also muscle; grace, but also momentum.

It arrives with energy, craft, and elegance — making its case in the most convincing way possible: by being excellent.

The piece moves with the clarity associated with the Mendelssohn sound world: quicksilver strings, buoyant rhythms, lyrical wind writing, a controlled exuberance that never spills into chaos. But Fanny's voice is not an echo of Felix's. Her writing has its own confidence, architecture, and dramatic pacing.

— Melissa Minkow

Notes on the Program

Cognōscent (2026)

Michael A. Lee

I was invited to write a piece specifically for this program, sandwiched between Fanny and Felix. As I marinated for a bit in both pieces, I reflected on the history of the Christian faith and the Christian church, and the ways in which these are sometimes in conflict.

Cognōscent builds on two primary motifs: one poses a question, the other proposes an answer. The first is drawn from the 13th-century chant Media Vita in Morte Sumus — in the midst of life we are in death — asking, "How should we who are dying then live?"

The second movement offers one possible answer: the Church Militant, seizing political and cultural power in the face of mortality, building kingdoms here on earth. But the pursuit grows both buffoonish and menacing, and ultimately fails to resolve.

It decays into the Chorale Mortis, a woodwind choir developing the Media Vita until it yields a different answer: the three-note motif (E–G–A) first introduced by the high bassoon. Derived from the folk hymn "They Will Know We Are Christians by Our Love," it repeats relentlessly in the low brass and strings, insistent even as the harmony stretches into bitonality.

How shall we who are dying then live? By love.
— Michael A. Lee

Symphony No. 5, Op. 107“Reformation” (1830)

Felix Mendelssohn

Felix Mendelssohn was only 20 when he embarked on his "Reformation" Symphony, intended for the 300th anniversary of the Augsburg Confession, a defining document of Lutheran theology presented in 1530.

Although numbered as his Fifth, the "Reformation" was actually one of Mendelssohn's earlier symphonic works — the numbering reflects publication history, not composition. He completed it in 1830; it was not published until after his death.

The symphony opens with a solemn, church-like introduction including the famous "Dresden Amen," a rising liturgical phrase later made famous by Wagner in Parsifal. Mendelssohn uses it not as an accent but as an entire theme. From there the first movement breaks into a fiery Allegro, full of urgency and contrapuntal drive.

The second movement brings contrast — nimble, bright, classically balanced. The third turns inward, singing with lyrical restraint. The finale brings the central symbol into full view: Luther's chorale “Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott.”

Mendelssohn collects the building materials, designs the architecture, and erects the cathedral.
— Melissa Minkow
